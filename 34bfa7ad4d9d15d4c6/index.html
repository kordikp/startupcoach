Security testing and runtime protection for AI systems

Can we develop an automated platform that (1) generates novel adversarial prompts to discover unknown vulnerabilities in deployed LLMs, (2) detects and prevents adversarial inputs in real time with very low latency and minimal false positives, (3) evaluates the security of LLM-based agents and their tool access, and (4) automatically maps security testing outputs to regulatory compliance artifacts? The research asks whether these capabilities can be achieved reliably given constraints on coverage, latency, verifiability and auditability.

📊 Market intelligence

Web-researched for this venture

Three things have moved since the June survey, and each one narrows where this venture can win. First, runtime guardrails are now a feature, not a company. At least fifteen AI-security start-ups were bought in a little over two years — Protect AI by Palo Alto Networks for $634.5M [1], Prompt Security by SentinelOne [2], CalypsoAI by F5 for $180M [3], Lakera by Check Point [4], Pangea by CrowdStrike [5] — and in 2026 the buyers stopped being only security vendors: OpenAI bought Promptfoo [6] and the legal-AI company Harvey bought Guardrails AI [7]. Underneath them the hyperscalers sell the same check for $0.15 per thousand text units [8], and Meta and NVIDIA give it away. Second, nobody's published accuracy survives contact with somebody else's data. Meta reports its Prompt Guard 2 detector at 97.5% recall at 1% false positives [9]; an independent 2026 study measured the same model letting 38.5% of attacks through, and its defences leaked three times as much when the prompts were not in English [10]. Third, 'evidence' is not empty space either: Gartner published its first Magic Quadrant for AI governance platforms in 2026 [11], and Giskard in Paris already runs a test-then-guard loop for BPCE and BNP Paribas [12]. What is left is narrower and more defensible than the June wedge: independent, per-deployment testing in the customer's own language, delivered in the shape DORA already demands — Article 24 obliges a bank to test every system behind a critical function at least yearly, through a party independent of it, and to prove that each finding was fixed [13]. Gartner sizes the whole 'securing AI' market at about $4.8B in 2027, up 68.7% in a year [14]. The Czech part of it is four named banks. That is a reference market, not a company — the plan has to say where the fifth customer comes from.

Why nowThe obligation is already in force, and it is not the one most pitches cite. DORA has applied since 17 January 2025: Article 24 requires a financial entity to run appropriate tests at least yearly on every ICT system supporting a critical or important function, to have them undertaken by independent parties, and to classify, remedy and re-validate every issue found [13]; Articles 28 and 30 add the register of ICT providers, audit rights, processing locations and an exit strategy [15] [16]. The AI Act is the weaker hook. The Digital Omnibus, in force since July 2026, moved the Annex III high-risk duties to 2 December 2027 and Annex I to 2 August 2028 [17] [18], and a customer-service assistant is not an Annex III use case at all — credit scoring and life or health insurance pricing are [19]. What did start to apply on 2 August 2026 is Article 50: a client must be told they are talking to a machine [20]. Meanwhile the exposure became real. ČSOB has been rolling out the LLM generation of Kate since April 2026 and is approaching half a million clients [21]; in April 2026 Microsoft and Salesforce patched prompt-injection flaws in Copilot Studio and Agentforce through which data left by way of a public form field [22] [23]; and the EBA's spring 2026 risk report names frontier language models as a growing cyber risk for banks [24]. Budgets follow: Gartner expects spending on securing AI to grow 68.7% in 2027 [14].
PositioningDo not position against the platforms on detection. Lakera Guard leads Lakera's own PINT benchmark at 95.2%, Bedrock Guardrails and Azure Prompt Shields sit near 89% [25], and all of them come bundled into contracts the bank already has. Position on the two things the vendor of a control cannot be: independent of it, and specific to one deployment. Independence is a legal word here — DORA Article 24(4) wants tests 'undertaken by independent parties' [13] — and it is getting scarcer as the testing tools are absorbed by the companies whose products they test: Lakera Red sits inside Check Point [26], Promptfoo inside OpenAI [6]. Specificity is an empirical word: Meta's own model card says prompt attacks are 'highly application-dependent' and recommends fine-tuning on application data, and it reports results for eight languages, none of them Czech [27]; Lakera's benchmark lists two dozen non-English languages, also without Czech [25]. This forces a choice the current pitch avoids: a company cannot be both the proxy in the request path and the independent party that tests it. The closest comparable is Giskard in Paris — an open-source core, a paid Hub, runtime Guards and an 'AI audit' that ends in a go/no-go report, sold to BPCE and BNP Paribas, with its researchers spending several weeks on one bank's chatbot [12] [28]. That is proof that EU banks buy this from a small specialist, and it is the competitor to name before a mentor does.
Whitespace / wedgeThe open ground is small and specific: Czech- and Slovak-language adversarial testing of one bank's live assistant, reported as the artefact DORA Article 24 asks for — a dated record of what was attempted, what got through, what was wrongly blocked, what was fixed, and the regression run proving the fix held on the next model version [13]. Three measured gaps support it. Over-defence: on NotInject, 339 benign prompts that merely contain trigger words, no open-source guard model scored above 60%, where 50% is a coin flip [29] — and a bank's security and compliance staff write exactly such text all day. Indirect injection: ProtectAI's widely used DeBERTa detector caught 58% of the injections hidden in e-mails and 4% of those hidden in tables [30]. Language: the same defence pipeline let through 25.1% of English attacks and 73.5% of Portuguese ones [10]. None of the three is closed by buying a platform licence, and each is cheap to demonstrate on a prospect's own traffic. What is not open: a horizontal guardrail API; an 'AI Act compliance platform' — IBM, ServiceNow, OneTrust and Credo AI are already in Gartner's quadrant [11]; or generic English red teaming, where the open-source tools are free and one of them, Promptfoo, now belongs to OpenAI [6].

✅ Strategic recommendations

  • Decide first whether DESET is the control or the independent tester of the control — the evidence says it cannot credibly be both. DORA Article 24(4) asks for independent parties [13]; if you sell the proxy, your red-team report is a vendor's self-assessment. The tester's seat is the one getting scarcer.
  • Build the measurement before the product: a Czech/Slovak attack set and a hard-negative set (incident reports, fraud policies, quoted attack strings), then publish your own operating point — recall at 1% false positives with p50/p95 latency — next to the public numbers for Prompt Guard 2, ProtectAI DeBERTa and NeMo Guardrails [9] [10] [31]. That table is the demo, the pitch and the first article in one.
  • Open every conversation with a bank on Article 24, not on the AI Act: 'you must test this system yearly, through somebody independent, and show that each finding was fixed — here is what that looks like for an LLM assistant' [13]. Give the AI Act one sentence: Article 50 since August 2026, high-risk not before December 2027 and not for a service chatbot [18] [19].
  • Price expert time, not API calls. The check itself is anchored at $0.15 per thousand text units [8] and at zero for the open-source models; an AI red-team engagement is quoted at roughly $25–50k and continuous testing at a few thousand a month [32] [33]. Those are vendors' own figures — verify with real quotes. First deal: a fixed-price assessment of one assistant with the re-test included.
  • Treat the four Czech banks as references, not as the market: ČSOB (Kate), Česká spořitelna (Hey George), Air Bank (Aneta) and Komerční banka (Ruby) [34] [35]. Before you pitch any of them, write down where customers five to twenty come from — Slovakia, and the KBC, Erste and Société Générale groups these banks belong to, are the natural route.
  • Test a channel hypothesis: Aneta is built by a Czech conversational-AI vendor, AddAI.Life [36]. A vendor with several deployments has to pass each bank's ICT third-party due diligence [15] and is one integration for many end customers. Two discovery calls with chatbot vendors will tell you whether they would buy testing or resell it.
  • Name Giskard in your own deck. Read what they sell — Hub, Guards, continuous red teaming, an audit ending in a go/no-go report and a label [12] — and be able to say in one sentence what a bank in Prague gets from you that it does not get from them. 'Czech, on site, DORA-shaped' is an answer; 'we also run a loop' is not.
  • Keep the acquisition logic but read it correctly: buyers pay for runtime technology and teams (Protect AI $634.5M [1], CalypsoAI $180M [3]), and independents are still being funded — HiddenLayer raised $100M in September 2026 [37]. An assurance practice is a services business first; it becomes acquirable only if the attack corpus and the regression tooling are a product in their own right.

⚠️ Key risks

  • Giskard, or a Big Four practice, sells the same thing to the same banks first. Giskard already lists BPCE and BNP Paribas and installs on-premise with its own engineers [12]; the Czech banks are subsidiaries of Belgian, Austrian and French groups that can simply reuse a group-level supplier.
  • The market is four names. A Czech-only plan has no path to a company; if neither the group-level route nor the chatbot-vendor channel opens, this is a consultancy.
  • The control vendors add 'evidence' as a feature. Cisco AI Defense advertises compliance mapping to NIST, OWASP and MITRE ATLAS and EU AI Act readiness [38] [39]; Check Point bought an LLM-evaluation company, Deepchecks, in 2026 [40]. A template is not a measurement of one deployment, but a buyer may not see the difference.
  • Independence cuts both ways. An independent tester gives up the recurring runtime revenue, and the subscription story shrinks to annual re-tests unless the regression suite genuinely runs continuously.
  • The headline numbers are fragile. The trade-off figures in the deck come from one synthetic 480-prompt study in a different domain [10]; PINT is Lakera's own benchmark on a private dataset [25]; Meta's 97.5% is measured on a private set too [27]. Quote shapes, not decimals, and replace them with your own measurement as soon as it exists.
  • DORA can keep you out as easily as let you in. Once you support a critical function you are an ICT third-party provider yourself, with audit rights, exit terms and due diligence that a two-person company has to survive [15] [16].
  • Regulatory timing slips exactly where pitches lean on it: the AI Act high-risk date has already moved sixteen months [17]. A pitch resting on it gets corrected by the first compliance officer in the room.
  • Pricing evidence is thin. The engagement prices above come from vendors' own blog posts [32]; nobody has told you what a Czech bank pays. Ask in discovery, do not assume.

🎯 What guardrails actually achieve: published operating points 9 src

Every published number is true on its own data and does not transfer. The same open detector is reported at 97.5% recall by its vendor and at 61.5% by an independent study. That spread, not any single figure, is the argument for measuring one deployment.

  • Prompt Guard 2 (86M), vendor: 97.5% recall at 1% false positives on English, AUC 0.998, 92.4 ms per classification on an A100 GPU at 512 tokens — on a private benchmark. The 22M variant: 88.7% recall, 19.3 ms.
  • Prompt Guard 2 (86M), independent (Maiorano, May 2026; 480 synthetic prompts, a programming tutor, English and Portuguese): 38.48% of attacks bypass it at a 3.60% false-positive rate, 74 ms average.
  • The same study, NeMo Guardrails: 'balanced' rail 0% bypass but 16.22% false positives, 1.47 s average and 4.75 s at p95; 'permissive' rail 2.44% bypass and 0% false positives, still about 1.7 s. The LLM-based rail is accurate and too slow for a chat window.
  • The same study, language gap: under the authors' own layered pipeline 25.1% of English attacks got through and 73.5% of Portuguese ones.
  • ProtectAI DeBERTa-v3 v2 (the most used open detector): 79.1% on PINT; recall 0.83 at about 3% false positives on Red Hat's EvalHub set (Oct 2026); recall 0.58 on injections hidden in e-mails and 0.04 on those hidden in tables (Mozilla.ai, BIPIA, Nov 2025).
  • Mozilla.ai also found the opposite failure: Deepset and Jasper detectors reach recall 1.0 on the same data with precision of only 0.63–0.78 — they flag everything.
  • Over-defence (InjecGuard, NotInject: 339 benign prompts containing trigger words): none of the open-source guard models tested exceeded 60% accuracy, where 50% is random.
  • Lakera's PINT (4,314 inputs, 20.9% of them hard negatives, private dataset): Lakera Guard 95.2%, Bedrock Guardrails 89.2%, Azure Prompt Shields 89.1%, ProtectAI v2 79.1%, Prompt Guard 2 (86M) 78.8%, Google Model Armor 70.1%. A vendor's benchmark, won by the vendor.
  • Agents (LlamaFirewall on AgentDojo): attack success falls from 17.6% to 7.5% with Prompt Guard 2 and to 1.75% with an added alignment check — while task utility falls from 47.7% to 42.7%. Safety is paid for in usefulness.
  • Latency: classifiers 19–92 ms; LLM-as-judge roughly 190–310 ms median in Red Hat's benchmark; NeMo's LLM rail 1.5 s and more.
  • Language coverage: Prompt Guard 2 reports results for English, French, German, Hindi, Italian, Portuguese, Spanish and Thai; PINT covers two dozen non-English languages. Czech is in neither list. Meta's card itself says attacks are 'highly application-dependent' and recommends fine-tuning on application data.
  • A vendor-run comparison (DKnownAI, Apr 2026) claims 96.5% recall against Bedrock, Azure and Lakera — one more number that is true on its author's data.

⚖️ Regulation: what is actually in force in November 2026 11 src

DORA is the obligation; the AI Act is mostly a date in the future. For a bank's customer-facing assistant the relevant duties are DORA Articles 24, 28 and 30, plus the AI Act's Article 50 disclosure rule.

  • DORA has applied since 17 January 2025. Art. 24(6): at least yearly, appropriate tests on all ICT systems and applications supporting critical or important functions.
  • Art. 24(4): tests must be 'undertaken by independent parties, whether internal or external'; with an internal tester, conflicts of interest must be avoided through design and execution.
  • Art. 24(5): procedures to prioritise, classify and remedy all issues revealed by testing, and internal validation that every weakness was fully addressed. This is, almost word for word, attack–measure–fix–regress.
  • Art. 28 and 30: a register of ICT third-party arrangements, exit strategies for services supporting critical functions, and contract terms naming processing locations and audit rights. A supplier of testing or guardrails to a bank is itself inside this regime.
  • The ESAs designated 19 critical ICT third-party providers on 18 November 2025, among them AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft, Oracle, SAP and Deutsche Telekom.
  • AI Act, already applying: obligations for general-purpose AI models since 2 August 2025; Article 50 disclosure duties (a person must be told they are interacting with an AI system) since 2 August 2026.
  • AI Act, moved: the Digital Omnibus — provisional agreement 7 May 2026, Parliament 16 June, Council 29 June, in force late July 2026 — set Annex III high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 and Annex I to 2 August 2028. Machine-readable marking for generative systems already on the market applies from 2 December 2026.
  • A customer-service assistant is not an Annex III high-risk use. In financial services Annex III covers creditworthiness evaluation and credit scoring, and risk assessment and pricing for life and health insurance.
  • Under the Omnibus the national authorities keep competence for AI systems in financial institutions — in practice the ČNB, which set up a dedicated AI department with effect from 1 August 2026.
  • The EBA's spring 2026 Risk Assessment Report (18 June 2026) flags frontier language models as a growing source of cyber risk for banks.
  • For banks DORA is the sector-specific regime; NIS2 and the Czech Cybersecurity Act matter for the next segment (energy, telecoms, health, public sector), not for the first one.

🚨 Incidents: it has already happened in production 4 src

Three disclosed cases in twelve months, all at vendors with large security teams, all the same shape: untrusted text reaches an assistant that holds data, and the data leaves.

  • EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711, CVSS 9.3), June 2025 — found by Aim Labs in Microsoft 365 Copilot: a zero-click attack in which a crafted e-mail made the assistant exfiltrate data from its context without any user action.
  • ForcedLeak (CVSS 9.4), September 2025 — found by Noma Labs in Salesforce Agentforce: an indirect prompt injection submitted through a public Web-to-Lead form, exfiltrating CRM data through an expired trusted domain.
  • April 2026 — Capsule Security disclosed prompt-injection flaws in Microsoft Copilot Studio (reported as CVE-2026-21520, CVSS 7.5) and Salesforce Agentforce; again a public form field was the entry point. Reporting notes that data was exfiltrated even where safety mechanisms flagged the attack.
  • The pattern matters more than the count: each time the entry point was content the organisation invites from outside — an e-mail, a form — which is exactly what a bank's assistant reads.
  • No disclosed incident involves a Czech or a named European bank. Do not imply otherwise in a pitch.

💶 Pricing anchors 4 src

The automated check is priced near zero; expert time is what the market pays for. Treat the engagement figures as vendor-published orientation, not as quotes.

  • Amazon Bedrock Guardrails: $0.15 per 1,000 text units for content filters and denied topics since 1 December 2024, after a cut of up to 85%. One text unit is up to 1,000 characters; each enabled policy is billed separately.
  • At that price a million assistant messages a month cost a bank on the order of $150 per policy. No start-up can build a business on undercutting it.
  • Open-source detectors and frameworks (Prompt Guard 2, LlamaFirewall, NeMo Guardrails, ProtectAI DeBERTa) cost only the compute to run them.
  • A manual AI red-team engagement: about $30–50k on average (Aona, 2026) or $25–80k per annual engagement depending on scope (SFAI Labs) — both vendor-published.
  • Continuous platform testing: AU$3,000–15,000 a month in the one published figure.
  • Giskard publishes no price for its Hub; BPCE reports several weeks of researcher time on one chatbot — a labour cost consistent with the engagement range above.
