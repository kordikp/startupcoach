Two things this survey told you in August do not survive a second look, and three things have changed since. Correction one: "nobody covers tracking, registry and serving on the customer's own machine without Kubernetes" is false. ClearML's open-source server installs with docker-compose on a single Linux host [1] and its serving component runs the same way [2]; a Valohai on-premises worker is a systemd service on an Ubuntu machine, with no cluster [3]; and MLflow has done all three on one machine for years [4]. Correction two: "MLflow has no access control" — the row in your own comparison table — has been out of date since May, when MLflow 3.13 shipped roles, workspace-scoped permissions and an admin interface for a self-hosted server [5]. What changed: on 30 September Weights & Biases became a product inside CoreWeave Forge, and its SaaS users now sign in through a GPU cloud's console [6]; TrueFoundry replaced the $499 and $2,999 plans quoted in August with per-user pricing for an LLM gateway [7]; and Neptune's users have had eight months to settle, with eight destinations listed on the official transition page — one of them a tracker launched for the occasion — and MolaBoard not among them [8]. So the gap is not architectural. It is commercial: a team of five to twenty people cannot buy a supported self-hosted install from anybody without a sales call. ClearML sells on-premises only in its Enterprise tier [9], Comet likewise [10], Valohai quotes every customer individually [11], and the self-serve plan of W&B is SaaS and limited to companies with fewer than 50 employees [12]. That is ground MolaBoard can hold — if it ships, and if the price is on the website. Regulatory citations and revenue figures carried over from the August survey were link-checked for this version, not re-read.

PositioningRemove both claims that are no longer true — "the only one without Kubernetes" and "MLflow has no access control" — before somebody who knows the field reads the table. What remains true and is rare: one product from the training run to the running endpoint, with promotion by stage, immutable revisions and exact rollback, bought per seat with a card. The nearest equivalents each fail a small team somewhere else. ClearML's free server is several services you operate yourself: the install guide has you change a kernel parameter for Elasticsearch and warns that the server starts with unrestricted access [1], and the supported on-premises version is an Enterprise quote [9]. Valohai needs credentials from its support team, a queue machine and an object store before the first worker starts [3], and publishes no price [11]. MLflow gives you tracking, a registry and a serving command, and leaves what connects them — which version is live on which machine, who promoted it, how to go back — to your own scripts [4]. dstack runs endpoints on your own servers over SSH with no cluster, and has no registry and no tracking [18]. The sentence to test: "From a training run to a running endpoint on your own GPU box, with a rollback button and a price on the website." One decision the positioning hangs on is still open from August: is MolaBoard a hosted control plane with the customer's machines attached, or software the customer installs entirely on their side? The first is the shape ClearML and Valohai already have, and it cannot be sold to an air-gapped or classified site. The second can, and is more work.

⚠️ Key risks

  • Two of the claims in your public description are now wrong — no Kubernetes as a unique property, and MLflow without access control. A reader who knows the field will find both in the first minute, and will then discount the rest.
  • Free keeps getting better. MLflow added workspaces in 3.10 (February 2026) and full role-based access control with an admin interface in 3.13 (29 May 2026) [5], and it is downloaded 19.9 million times a month [14]. Every release removes a reason to pay for a tracker.
  • A year of building without an outside user. In the months MolaBoard stayed private, a new tracker was launched for exactly the users Neptune left behind, got itself listed on the official transition page next to seven other destinations [8], and charges $250 a seat [19].
  • Willingness to pay at small-team scale is unproven. At published per-seat prices a ten-seat team is worth between $1,800 and $30,000 a year — an order of magnitude below the $50–200k contract value the August sizing assumed.
  • The architecture decision cuts both ways. A hosted control plane disqualifies air-gapped, classified and most defence sites [23]; a fully installable product multiplies the support load on a single founder.
  • The neighbours are one release away. dstack lacks a registry and tracking [18]; ClearML lacks a self-serve on-premises price [9]. Either gap closes with one release or one pricing page.
  • You are a manufacturer under the Cyber Resilience Act as soon as you sell software that installs on a customer's machine in the EU. Reporting of actively exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours has applied since 11 September 2026; the full obligations follow on 11 December 2027 [24].
⚔️ Who already runs tracking, registry and serving on your own machine — without Kubernetes 13 src

Three products do, and a fourth does half. The August survey said none did; that was wrong, and the correction changes what MolaBoard can claim. What separates them from each other is not what they can do but what it takes to start, and who you have to talk to first.

  • ClearML Server (open source) deploys with docker-compose on a single Linux host: at least 8 GB of memory, ports 8080, 8081 and 8008, and a kernel parameter change for Elasticsearch. The guide warns that the server launches with unrestricted access until you secure it.
  • ClearML Serving installs with pip and runs its containers with docker-compose; the prerequisites read 'Kubernetes / Single-instance Machine', and the Helm chart is optional. Licence Apache-2.0. Together with the server that is tracking, registry and serving on one box, with no cluster.
  • Valohai on-premises: the worker agent ('Peon') runs as a systemd service on Ubuntu 24.04 with Docker and the NVIDIA Container Toolkit. No Kubernetes. It needs a queue address, a Redis password and an installer URL from Valohai's support team; the worker talks to a queue machine inside the customer's environment (Redis over TLS), and artifacts stay in the customer's object storage. Valohai never connects to the on-premises machine directly.
  • That Valohai design is MolaBoard's 'outbound connections only, model files never pass through us' almost word for word. It is not a property you can claim as unique.
  • MLflow (Apache-2.0, 28,762 GitHub stars): tracking, a registry with versions and aliases, and 'mlflow models serve' for a local scoring endpoint. Since 3.10 it has workspaces, and since 3.13 (29 May 2026) role-based access control with reusable roles, workspace-scoped grants and an admin interface, switched on by starting the server with authentication enabled.
  • dstack (Munich, MPL-2.0, 2,310 stars): 'SSH fleets' turn existing servers into a pool with nothing but Linux, Docker and SSH — no cluster. Its services are inference endpoints with token authentication on by default, in-place rolling updates and optional autoscaling. It has no model registry and no experiment tracking. It was missing from the August survey.
  • SkyPilot (Apache-2.0, 10,821 stars, 1.1 million downloads a month) covers the same orchestration ground across clouds and on-premises machines and raised a $20M seed in July 2026. Like dstack it schedules work and does not track or register models.
  • Weights & Biases covers tracking and registry; since 30 September 2026 it is a product inside CoreWeave Forge, where training, inference and the registry are CoreWeave services. The pricing page offers private or dedicated deployment only on the Enterprise plan.
  • What none of them offers a small team: a supported install on the team's own hardware that can be bought without a sales conversation. ClearML and Comet keep on-premises in the Enterprise tier, Valohai quotes individually, and MLflow and dstack are free and unsupported.
  • Usage on 11 November 2026, PyPI downloads in the last month: MLflow 19.9M, wandb 14.5M, SkyPilot 1.13M, ClearML 0.77M, comet-ml 0.33M, trackio 0.31M, ZenML 0.20M, BentoML 0.13M, dstack 0.015M. The August survey quoted 40.3M for MLflow; that figure does not reproduce today and should not be reused.

  • ClearML: Community free for up to 3 users; Pro $15 per user per month for up to 10 users, plus metered overages — $0.10 per GB of artifacts, $0.01 per MB of metric events, $1 per 100,000 API calls. Scale is a quote and VPC only; on-premises and air-gapped exist only in Enterprise.

  • Weights & Biases (CoreWeave Forge): Free up to 5 seats and 5 GB; Pro 'starts at $60/month' for up to 10 seats and 100 GB — and is intended for companies with fewer than 50 employees; larger companies go to Enterprise by quote. No self-hosted option on Free or Pro.

🏦 The first twenty customers: four segments, each with a count and a reason to buy 10 src

Your README lists research labs, computer vision and robotics, manufacturing, medical and defence. They are not equal. One has a legal reason to buy, one has a commercial reason, one will use the product and not pay, and one can only buy a product you have not decided to build.

  • Medical imaging vendors — the legal reason. The Health AI Register lists 310 CE-marked radiology AI products (11 November 2026): 203 certified under the MDR and 107 still on MDD certificates. Regulation (EU) 2023/607 lets those legacy certificates run until 31 December 2027 or 31 December 2028 depending on device class, on condition that the manufacturer is moving to the MDR.
  • For those vendors a model update is a change to an approved device, which needs the notified body's approval where it could affect safety or performance (MDR Annex IX, 4.10), and technical documentation has to be kept for at least ten years (Art. 10(8)). Who retrained what, from which code and data, and which version was in service when, is something they must be able to show.
  • Mid-size companies with an ML team — the commercial reason. A company with 50 or more employees cannot stay on the self-serve plan of W&B, and the self-serve plans of ClearML and Comet stop at 10 users. A team of 5–20 in such a company is pushed into an enterprise sales process for a tracker. No count exists for this segment; five interviews will tell you more than any estimate.
  • Research labs — users, not revenue. EuroHPC lists 19 AI Factories and 13 antennas, a Czech one among them, and university groups own GPU machines everywhere. But the leading commercial tracker is free for academics up to 100 seats, MLflow is free for everybody, and the AI Act does not apply to research.
  • Defence and dual-use — only with a fully installable product. The 2025 European Defence Fund calls selected 57 projects involving 634 entities. Systems handling EU classified information need formal accreditation, which a vendor-hosted control plane cannot pass, and the AI Act does not apply to defence. Without an install that runs with no connection to you, this segment is closed.
  • The teams Neptune left behind are no longer a segment. The service ended on 5 March 2026 and eight destinations were waiting for them.
  • What is still unknown for every segment: who signs. In a medical vendor it may be quality and regulatory affairs rather than the ML lead. Ask it in the first interview.

🧾 The release record as the product: which clauses ask for what MolaBoard already stores 7 src

For a regulated buyer the registry and the deployment log are not conveniences; they are evidence. This section maps what your product records onto the clauses that ask for it, and names what is missing.

  • MDR Annex IX, 4.10: changes to an approved device require approval from the notified body where they could affect safety and performance. What MolaBoard holds: an immutable revision per deployment and a record of every stage change with the person and the time.
  • MDR Art. 10(8): technical documentation is kept available for at least 10 years after the last device was placed on the market, 15 for implantables. What MolaBoard does not yet say: how long a release record is kept, and how the customer gets it out.
  • MDR Annex I, 17.2: software is developed according to the state of the art, taking into account the development life cycle, risk management, verification and validation. What MolaBoard holds: the commit, the uncommitted changes as a patch, the hyperparameters and the console output of each run — enough to reproduce a model.
  • MDCG 2025-6 (June 2025): a medical AI system can keep one set of technical documentation for the MDR and the AI Act, and changes the manufacturer planned and documented in advance are not a substantial modification. A registry that records the planned change and the executed one side by side is what that guidance asks for.
  • AI Act Art. 12 requires automatic logging over the lifetime of a high-risk system, Annex IV a description of the changes made through its life cycle, and Art. 17 written procedures for managing modifications. These apply from 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (products under Annex I, medical devices included).
  • Missing for an auditor: an export of the release record as a file that can be filed and signed; a stated retention period; and dataset versions, which app.md lists as a placeholder. Without the data a model was trained on, the record explains the release and not the model.
  • The MDR is being revised (procedure 2025/0404(COD)): about 800 amendments were tabled in Parliament in July 2026, the committee vote is scheduled for 3 December, and the Council aims at a general approach by the end of the year. The proposal would move medical devices to Section B of Annex I of the AI Act, so AI in devices would be governed mainly by the MDR. Until it is adopted, the current MDR applies.
Sources: [22][41][42][43][44][16][45]
⚖️ Regulation: the MDR is in force, the AI Act is not yet, and the Cyber Resilience Act now applies to you 7 src

One regulation gives a customer a reason to buy today, one is two years away, and one — new since the August survey — puts obligations on MolaBoard itself.

  • In force for the customer: the MDR, applicable since 2021, with the change-approval and record-keeping duties set out in the section above.
  • Not yet in force: the AI Act's high-risk obligations, postponed by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 to 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for products under Annex I. A pitch that says 'AI Act compliance' is selling a 2028 problem.
  • Out of scope of the AI Act altogether: systems used exclusively for military, defence or national security purposes (Art. 2(3)) and systems developed solely for scientific research and development (Art. 2(6)). Two of your five target segments have no AI Act reason to buy.
  • What moves those two segments instead: GDPR Chapter V, which makes any processing outside the EU a documented transfer assessment, and Council Decision 2013/488/EU, which requires accreditation of systems handling EU classified information.
  • New and on your side of the table: the Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847). Since 11 September 2026 manufacturers of products with digital elements must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents to the national CSIRT and ENISA within 24 hours, including for products already on the market. The remaining obligations — secure development, vulnerability handling, documentation, CE marking — apply from 11 December 2027.
  • Software you sell to be installed on a customer's machine is such a product. The Commission published guidance on the Act's application on 27 July 2026; read the part on remote data processing before you decide how much of MolaBoard runs on your side.
  • For a regulated or public buyer the same Act is a question in the tender: who is the manufacturer, how do we learn about a vulnerability, and for how long are fixes provided. Have the three answers written down.
Sources: [22][16][17][46][23][47][24]

🌊 Why workloads move to owned compute — and which arguments do not hold 6 src

The move is real among large enterprises and driven by control and cost predictability. Whether it reaches the small teams MolaBoard is built for is not shown by any survey.

  • Broadcom Private Cloud Outlook 2026 (Radius Tech for Broadcom; 1,800 senior IT decision-makers at organisations with 1,000 or more employees in eight countries; fielded February–March 2026): 56% run or plan to run production AI inference on private cloud; public cloud's share fell 15 points to 41%.
  • Same survey: 83% are considering moving workloads from public to private cloud and 50% have moved at least some; cost has overtaken security as the first concern about public cloud, rising from 26% to 31%.
  • Two caveats. Broadcom sells the private cloud software, and the 83% counts companies, not workloads: one small application moved back is enough to be counted.
  • The tracker market itself is being tied to compute. W&B is now part of a GPU cloud's platform, Anyscale is being bought by another, and Replicate belongs to Cloudflare. For a team that owns its machines, a tool sold by a compute vendor has an obvious conflict of interest — this is the form of the sovereignty argument that applies to you.
  • An argument that does not hold: price. As recorded in August, the large clouds cut GPU rental prices sharply in 2025, and owning a card pays back only at high, sustained utilisation. Sell control and continuity, not savings.
  • An argument that does not hold either: 'everybody is leaving W&B'. 14.5 million downloads a month five weeks after the Forge launch says they are not.
